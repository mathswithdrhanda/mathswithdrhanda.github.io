1.3

The big idea

So far every velocity has been along one line, so a plus or minus sign was enough to show direction. Impulse and momentum are vectors, though, so when a body moves in two dimensions you write its velocity in the form ai + bj.

Nothing about the principles changes. The impulse–momentum principle becomes I = mv − mu, where u and v are now velocity vectors, and conservation of momentum becomes m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂. The working is simpler than it looks, because you can deal with the i parts and the j parts separately.

In the animations, the left panel shows the motion and the right panel shows the momentum vectors drawn nose to tail: the old momentum mu plus the impulse I gives the new momentum mv.

Example 8

Finding the new velocity

A particle of mass 0.2 kg is moving with velocity (10i − 5j) m s−1 when it receives an impulse (3i − 2j) N s. Find the new velocity of the particle.
Example 9

An ice hockey puck

An ice hockey puck of mass 0.17 kg receives an impulse Q N s. Immediately before the impulse the velocity of the puck is (10i + 5j) m s−1 and immediately afterwards its velocity is (15i − 7j) m s−1. Find the magnitude of Q and the angle between Q and i.
Example 10

A squash ball hits a wall

A squash ball of mass 0.025 kg is moving with velocity (22i + 37j) m s−1 when it hits a wall. It rebounds with velocity (10i − 11j) m s−1. Find the impulse exerted by the wall on the squash ball.
Example 11

Two particles coalesce

A particle of mass 0.15 kg is moving with velocity (20i − 10j) m s−1 when it collides with a particle of mass 0.25 kg moving with velocity (16i − 8j) m s−1. The two particles coalesce and form one particle of mass 0.4 kg. Find the velocity of the combined particle.
