1.1

What momentum is

The momentum of a body is its mass multiplied by its velocity: p = mv. With mass in kg and velocity in m s−1, momentum comes out in kg m s−1. Since a newton is 1 kg m s−2, one kg m s−1 is the same as one newton second, so you can also write momentum in N s.

Velocity is a vector and mass is a scalar, so momentum is a vector too. Its sign tells you its direction.

1.1

What impulse is

If a constant force F acts for time t, the impulse of the force is I = Ft, measured in N s. Force is a vector and time is a scalar, so impulse is a vector.

Start from F = ma with a = (v − u)/t. Multiply both sides by t and you get Ft = mv − mu. Impulse equals final momentum minus initial momentum, which is called the impulse–momentum principle. A bat hitting a ball, or a string jerking tight, are impulses: the time is tiny and the force is large, so their product is a sensible size.

1.2

Conservation of momentum

When two bodies collide, Newton's third law says each pushes on the other with an equal and opposite force, for exactly the same length of time. So each receives an impulse of the same size in opposite directions. Their changes in momentum cancel out, and the total momentum of the system does not change.

Total momentum before impact = total momentum after impact, or m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂.

Example 4

P collides with Q at rest

A particle P of mass 2 kg is moving with speed 3 m s−1 on a smooth horizontal plane. Particle Q of mass 3 kg is at rest on the plane. P collides with Q and after the collision Q moves off with speed 73 m s−1. Find:
  1. the speed and direction of motion of P after the collision
  2. the magnitude of the impulse received by P in the collision.
Example 5

A head-on collision

Two particles A and B of masses 2 kg and 4 kg respectively are moving towards each other in opposite directions along the same straight line on a smooth horizontal surface. Before the collision the speeds of A and B are 3 m s−1 and 2 m s−1 respectively. After the collision the direction of motion of A is reversed and its speed is 2 m s−1. Find:
  1. the speed and direction of B after the collision
  2. the magnitude of the impulse given by A to B in the collision.
Example 6

The string goes taut

Two particles P and Q, of masses 8 kg and 2 kg respectively, are connected by a light inextensible string. The particles are at rest on a smooth horizontal plane with the string slack. Particle P is projected directly away from Q with speed 4 m s−1.
  1. Find the common speed of the particles after the string goes taut.
  2. Find the magnitude of the impulse transmitted through the string when it goes taut.
The sudden tightening of the string is called a jerk.
Example 7

Working back from the impulse

Two particles A and B of masses 2 kg and 4 kg respectively are moving towards each other in opposite directions along the same straight line on a smooth horizontal surface. The particles collide. Before the collision the speeds of A and B are 3 m s−1 and 2 m s−1 respectively. Given that the magnitude of the impulse due to the collision is 7 N s, find:
  1. the velocity of A after the collision
  2. the velocity of B after the collision.
